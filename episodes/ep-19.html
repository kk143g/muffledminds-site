About This Episode

In Episode 19 of Muffled Minds, host Khawar Shahzad sits down with Aimen Wasay, a speech and language therapist from Karachi settled in Lahore, to explain what speech therapy actually is: who needs it, when a child should be taken to a therapist, and why early intervention in the first three years matters so much.

Aimen explains how therapy works across ages, from newborns with feeding issues and cleft palate to teenagers with stammering and adults with aphasia after an accident, how progress is really measured over three to six months, and why the work parents do at home matters more than the hour at the centre. She also talks honestly about parents' expectations, denial, Google-based parenting, and the pressure parents put on therapists.

The conversation then turns to the side of therapy centres that is rarely discussed: long shifts, low starting salaries, cameras and surveillance, no off days, and the burnout that follows, and closes with what keeps her hopeful: small milestones, a parent who stays determined, and the belief that success is happiness and changing children's lives.

Key Takeaways

  • The ideal time to see a therapist is as soon as you first feel something is wrong, and early intervention between 1 and 3 years gives the best prognosis, because the child's mind is still developing.
  • Speaking is not only words: a child who communicates through gestures is also communicating, and progress is measured by sitting, response and understanding, not only by how much the child speaks.
  • Parents should wait three to six months and judge progress by visible change, and give the therapist three months with proper set goals before deciding to switch.
  • Therapy is a parent-led approach: the child is with the therapist for an hour, but the parent has the rest of the time, so the work at home matters more than the sessions.
  • Centres often push two or three therapies for business, but therapies should be prioritised by the child's need, not by the session count.
  • Therapists work 7 to 8 hour shifts for a starting salary package of 30 to 35, with no replacement, no paid offs and almost no perks, and the long hours reduce their efficiency.
  • Cameras in therapy centres show a lack of trust, and constant surveillance takes away the therapist's privacy, though they can be a lifesaver when a child is being mishandled.
  • Only five to seven percent of parents show appreciation to their therapist, yet a small gesture, even an Eid card with a chocolate, means a lot.
  • Success for Aimen is happiness: changing children's lives and meeting them after ten years, knowing they are better because of her.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is speech therapy?

Speech therapy teaches children and adults to speak and communicate. Aimen explains it covers newborns with feeding issues, children with autism, Down syndrome or cerebral palsy, teenagers with stammering, and adults who have lost speech after an accident or brain injury.

When should a child be taken to a speech therapist?

As soon as parents first feel something is wrong. Aimen says to observe the child for one or two weeks, and if milestones are delayed by more than six months, take the child to a therapist. The early intervention stage, from 1 to 3 years, gives the best results.

How long does speech therapy take to show progress?

Aimen gives an ideal time frame of three to six months for a visible change. Parents should judge progress by sitting, response, understanding and behaviour, not only by how many words the child speaks, and should give a therapist three months with proper set goals.

What is the difference between speech therapy, physio and behaviour therapy?

They should be prioritised by the child's need, not all given at once. If a child cannot walk properly, learning to walk matters more than speaking; if there are severe behaviour issues, behaviour is addressed first. A child who cannot speak can also be put on alternative communication.

Why do therapists burn out?

Aimen describes 7 to 8 hour shifts with 11 or 12 patients a day, a starting salary package of 30 to 35, no replacement when ill, no paid offs, almost no perks, and constant camera surveillance in centres, which together drain therapists mentally and physically.

Who is the guest in this episode?

Aimen Wasay, a speech and language therapist from Karachi, settled in Lahore. She holds a Master's in Speech, Language and Hearing Sciences from the University of Lahore, has worked formally full-time for four years, and now works in private centres as a pediatric speech therapist.
