About This Episode

In Episode 17 of Muffled Minds, host Khawar Shahzad sits down with Abdullah Sheikh, a COMSATS Lahore graduate who went from being a software engineer to building and running a software house with 40-50 employees, to talk about youth entrepreneurship in Pakistan: what happens after the degree, why most graduates chase jobs, and what it really takes to start a software house from zero.

Abdullah traces his own journey from a Sheikh business family in Lahore, where his father told him he could never do a job, through COMSATS Lahore in the spring 2011 batch to graduation in 2015, and then freelancing, because a good job did not come. He explains the gap between university and professional life, why bookish knowledge is not enough, and how internships, teamwork and learning in the market matter more than the degree alone. He then covers partnerships, written agreements and documentation, client hunting on platforms like Fiverr, Upwork and Freelancer.com, and the difference between local and foreign clients.

The conversation then turns to team management, the impact of AI and ChatGPT on developers and clients, the shortage of jobs for graduates in Pakistan, and his three golden pieces of advice for young entrepreneurs: skill, communication and commitment. It closes with what success means to him and the Fish Jar Q/A, where he talks about why Pakistan still has plenty of opportunities.

Key Takeaways

  • Start from zero: every software house starts with the founder doing everything himself, from opening the office to serving chai.
  • Learn freelancing first: spend two to four years freelancing before starting a software house, because it teaches you client communication, documentation and delivery.
  • Write everything down: partnerships, shares, effort and client requirements should all be documented, because verbal agreements lead to disputes.
  • Local clients and foreign clients are different: foreign clients work in a systemized way with clear lists and timelines, while local clients often have unclear expectations and limited budgets.
  • Commitment is everything: if you commit to delivering in a week or ten days, you have to deliver, and management without commitment is nothing.
  • AI saves time but juniors who depend on it completely stop learning: use AI as a helper, not as a replacement for basics.
  • There are far more graduates than jobs: universities pass out hundreds of students per batch while a software house can only hire one or two.
  • Skill, communication and commitment are the three golden advices: develop a skill, learn to communicate with clients, and deliver on your commitment.
  • Success is commitment: closing what you start and keeping your word is what success means, and opportunities in Pakistan have not ended, only their form has changed.
