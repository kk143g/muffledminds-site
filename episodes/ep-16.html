About This Episode

In Episode 16 of Muffled Minds, host Haris (Rana Haris Sajid) sits down with architect and designer Noorul Huda Khan from Lahore to talk about the stress of the dream home in Pakistan: why building your own house has become so difficult, what expectation vs reality looks like, and how weddings, social media and the housing market turn a home into a source of stress.

Noor explains what an architect actually does, why a three-marla house is never designed by an architect, and how the market works: designers show dreams, but dreams are not within everyone's reach, and selling dreams is not good business. He tells the story of the 15,000-rupee floor plan he made for Khawar bhai, why he charges for skill, and why he works mostly for foreign designers. He also talks about imposter syndrome, the toxic jobs and low pay he faced early in his career, and how he moved to freelancing.

The conversation then turns to why small houses in Pakistani cities feel unlivable: no rules and regulations, encroachments, ramps and cantilevers, and a policy framework that is never enforced. Noor and Haris discuss the system of bribes and demolitions, the 6 lakh children out of school, and whether anything can change from the top or only from the bottom. It closes with the comfort vs showcase question and a rapid-fire round, where Noor reveals his final-year thesis: houses for people who cannot build houses.

Key Takeaways

  • Reduce expectations and show reality: a designer has to be honest about what a budget can actually build.
  • A three-marla house is never designed by an architect: it is the lowest of the low cutting, and its owner will not pay for design.
  • Dreams are not within everyone's reach: designers show dreams, but a dream is not a product you can sell to someone who cannot pay for it.
  • Charge for skill: a floor plan is worth paying for because you know the dimensions, the software and how to explain it to a client.
  • Imposter syndrome is dealt with by not listening to everyone, keeping confidence, keep working and demanding your worth.
  • Foreign clients pay because they understand the value of design; local clients often do not, and the market pays for technical skills.
  • Small houses are unlivable because of policy, not design: with no rules enforced, everyone extends forward and no one checks.
  • Change will not come from the top: the bureaucrats will not listen, so the impact has to be made on the common people, one person at a time.
  • A house for show and a house for peace are both valid choices: blame no one for building the house they want.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is building a dream home so stressful in Pakistan?

Noor says parents spent a lifetime of hard work and savings to provide the house their children live in today, and building your own house now means meeting expectations that keep growing while budgets do not. Designers show dreams on Instagram and Pinterest, but those dreams are not within everyone's reach, and the gap between the dream and the budget is where the stress comes from.

Why is a three-marla house never designed by an architect?

Noor says a three-marla house is the lowest of the low cutting: its owner is building a house for the first time and will not spend money on a designer, because the culture says he will build it himself. The floor plans are all the same standard design: two rooms at the back, two bathrooms, a laundry area, a living area and a kitchen.

What is the story of the 15,000-rupee floor plan?

Khawar bhai came through a reference from Talha Shahid, and Noor quoted him 15,000 rupees for a floor plan. The market rate is around 250 to 300 rupees per square foot, which would have made it 80 to 90 lakh rupees, but Noor says he took 15,000 because that was very little and he would take whatever they gave. Khawar bhai saw the value and paid, because he knew he was paying for skill: knowing the dimensions, the software and how to explain it to a client.

What does Noor say about imposter syndrome?

He says it is the fear that you are not successful or that people will not consider you successful, even when you are surviving on your own skills. He did not face it himself because he was confident and left his jobs after five to six months to freelance, but he has counselled peers who did: do not listen to everyone, bring confidence, keep working and demand your worth, because you have the right to dream.

Why are small houses in Pakistani cities unlivable?

Noor says it is policy, not design. Three-marla houses are built in the standard way, but there are no rules and regulations and no one to enforce them, so everyone extends forward with slopes and cantilevers, streets get congested, sunlight disappears and gutters overflow. In New York, the city gives an incentive, an extra floor if you leave public space, but in Pakistan the policy framework exists and is simply not enforced.

Who is the guest in this episode?

Noorul Huda Khan, an architect and designer from Lahore. By profession he is an architect, and architects deal with the designing of the entire built environment, whether a house, urban design, an outdoor park or an interior. He works as a freelancer, mostly for foreign designers, and his final-year thesis was about building houses for people who cannot build houses.
