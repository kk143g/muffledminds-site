About This Episode

In Episode 4 of Muffled Minds, host Khawar Shahzad sits down with his friend Ameer Hamza, a DevOps engineer and cyber security expert from Lahore, to talk about the youth mental health crisis in Pakistan. They open with the numbers: 53% of high school students face anxiety or some kind of depression, most of them aged 15 to 18, and the pressures behind it — social media, home circumstances, finances and a personal struggle they absorb but cannot act on.

From there the conversation moves to who a young person can talk to — parents first, then siblings, friends and a spouse — and why that first discussion is the biggest step. They discuss the 2019 figures: 19,000 suicides in Pakistan, around 14,000 males and 4,500 females, mostly aged 16 to 28, and the triggers that push someone there: job issues, relationships, parents, and several problems arriving together. They also talk about normalising mental health talk, a counsellor in every school, and why teachers and principals should dedicate a lecture to it.

The last part turns to talent and the future: letting a child try many things, gaming and reading included, teaching him to take risks, and why starting to earn at 18 beats starting at 24. They discuss separating from parents without leaving them, why business should be separate from family, and why Pakistan cannot produce millionaires — a class jump takes a generation, and the youth, around 40% by the guest's estimate, leave for the same issues the episode is about.

Key Takeaways

  • Talking is the biggest step: anxiety and depression can happen to anyone, but discussing it with someone is what starts the way out.
  • Go to parents first, then siblings, then friends: if the bond with parents is good, their experience guides best; siblings are the underrated next best.
  • Do not label anxiety too much: it also teaches you, and the age of 15 to 18 is for learning through experience.
  • Problems arrive together: when three or four issues hit at once, solve them one by one, because each one peaks while you work on another.
  • Counselling should start in school: a counsellor in every school and a dedicated lecture slot would catch problems before they reach 80%.
  • Let children try many things: talent shows itself through gaming, reading and experiments, not through grades alone.
  • Start earning at 18: a child who starts at 18 has his own thinking by 24, while parents who shield him delay it.
  • Separate your life, not your parents: live separately and decide for yourself, while staying physically close and responsible.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do 53% of high school students have anxiety or depression?

Ameer Hamza says the age of 15 to 18 is when you absorb a lot but cannot act: social media, home circumstances, finances and a personal struggle, with no resources of your own. He adds that it should not be labelled too much, because that age is for learning, and anxiety also teaches you.

Who should a young person talk to about anxiety or depression?

Parents first, because their experience guides best if the bond is good; then siblings, who the hosts call very underrated; then friends; and if married, the spouse, who can never leave you the way a friend can. The first discussion is the biggest step, and it is what normalises mental health talk.

What did the episode say about suicides in Pakistan?

Ameer Hamza cites a survey from 2019: 19,000 suicides in Pakistan, around 14,000 males and 4,500 females, mostly aged 16 to 28. The triggers he lists are job issues, relationship issues, problems with parents, and several problems arriving together, with financial pressure and middle-class families the biggest share. The references are in the video description.

What should schools do about student mental health?

The hosts say every school should have a dedicated counsellor, and a dedicated lecture slot where students are trained. A principal can invite a person to talk about it, and teachers and stakeholders will get involved automatically. They add that a person must also be willing to share, rather than keeping quiet until the problem has grown to 80%.

Why does the episode say Pakistan cannot produce millionaires?

Ameer Hamza says a class jump takes a whole generation, and the youth leave for the same issues discussed in the episode: uncertainty, no opportunities to learn new technology, and a mindset fixed on going abroad. He estimates around 40% leave because of these issues, while others leave home or responsibility.

Ameer Hamza, a DevOps engineer and cyber security expert from Lahore, Gulberg, and a close friend of host Khawar Shahzad. He has been freelancing since graduation for different clients and is now learning AI.
